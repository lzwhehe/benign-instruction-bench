\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of results}
The results are consistent with a simple account. A detector learns to separate the injected inputs it was trained on from the benign inputs it was trained on, and both sides have a form: prompt-length text, emails and tables, or structured tool records with a planted sentence. On inputs of a familiar form, the detector separates well. On inputs of an unfamiliar form, two things go wrong at once: benign inputs land in regions the detector was never told are benign, which produces the false positives of RQ1, and injected inputs are diluted by surrounding text the detector was never told to ignore, which produces the misses of RQ2. The seen/unseen comparison of RQ3 isolates this effect. Benchmarks reward the same alignment: BIPIA's inputs have the form PIGuard was trained on, and AgentDojo's templates are explicit instructions addressed to the model, which resemble the prompt injections that prompt-trained detectors know. A benchmark score is therefore informative about deployment only to the extent that the benchmark's inputs have the form of the deployment's inputs.

\subsection{Reconciling with other measurements}
\label{sec:community}

\paragraph{The buried-injections benchmark}
A community benchmark released while this study was in preparation~\cite{buriedinjections} also scores open detectors on AgentDojo injections inside tool outputs (629 injected and 97 benign cases). It reports that Prompt Guard~2 catches 1\% of injections at its default threshold and, with the threshold recalibrated at a 2\% budget on three AgentDojo suites and tested on the fourth, 99\% pooled and 97\% in the worst suite at 5\% false positives. Our in-sample figure for the same model is \MPGtAD at 1\% FPR. The settings differ in five ways: the budget (1\% versus 2\%); the input (the user's task prompt followed by the tool output in overlapping 510-token windows, versus the tool output alone in consecutive 512-token segments); the benign set (97 prompt--output pairs versus \NCleanOutputs replayed outputs); the attack templates (only \texttt{important\_instructions}, which its authors note makes the result an upper bound, versus three); and the injection placement (an attack embedded in a benign tool output, versus AgentDojo's injection points in a replayed task).

We reproduced the benchmark's protocol on our rebuilt data for the first four differences. With the task prompt before the tool output, overlapping windows, and a 2\% budget calibrated on three suites, Prompt Guard~2 (86M) catches \CommTaskLoTpr of AD-Matched injections pooled and \CommTaskLoMin in the worst suite, at \CommTaskLoFpr false positives on held-out suites (up to \CommTaskLoMaxFpr in one suite); restricted to \texttt{important\_instructions}, it catches \CommTaskLoII pooled and \CommTaskLoIIMin in the worst suite. The false-positive rate matches the benchmark's, while detection stays well below 99\%. The attack template explains part of the difference: at the default threshold, the detector catches \CommTaskDefaultIgn of \texttt{ignore\_previous} injections, \CommTaskDefaultII of \texttt{important\_instructions} injections, and none of the \texttt{direct} injections, which state the attacker's goal without any framing. The rest presumably comes from the injection placement and the benign set, which we cannot replicate without the benchmark's cases.

Both studies find Prompt Guard~2's default threshold far too high for tool outputs and recalibration essential. They differ on how far recalibration goes: in our data, the recalibrated threshold transfers unevenly between suites (the benchmark reports the same for the 22M model), and detection depends strongly on the attack template, so we read the benchmark's 99\% as an upper bound for one template and one way of embedding attacks, as its authors present it. The ranking of Section~\ref{sec:rq2} is unaffected: the benchmark does not evaluate Horizon-Labs or Wolf Defender, and under our held-out protocol both stay above Prompt Guard~2 (Table~\ref{tab:heldout}).

\paragraph{Detector baselines in defense papers}
MELON~\cite{melon} reports a 61.86\% false-positive rate, counted per task, for a DeBERTa detector screening the tool outputs of GPT-4o on AgentDojo. ProtectAI v2, the detector AgentDojo ships, would stop \CleanPABlock of tasks on our replayed outputs. The two numbers come from different agents and tool-call sequences but describe the same failure. Biswas~\cite{biswas2026} finds that ProtectAI v2 and Prompt Guard~2 confidently pass injections that hijack the model's behavior without an explicit command, which is consistent with Prompt Guard~2 missing every \texttt{direct} injection at its default threshold and with task-irrelevant BIPIA injections evading most detectors (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).
